How we tested it

Only a lawyer can say whether a call is legally right. But a lot can be checked without one: does the tool look at everything, does it give the same answer twice, and do the rules around it do their job?

In short

Strong
It comments on 93% of the other side's edits to our template. Every quote it gives is real text from the contract.
Weak
It is not consistent. Five fresh reviewers raise the same issue only 39% to 52% of the time.
Fixed in Phase 2.1
The same issue now comes up in 93% to 100% of repeat reviews, and no tracked change is missed. See Phase 2.
Not tested
Legal accuracy. That needs a lawyer to mark a sample of answers right or wrong.

The test contracts

We wrote 30 made-up NDAs. Ten are on our own template, with the other side's changes marked as tracked changes. Twenty are on the other side's own paper. Between them they carry the usual requests: a shorter confidentiality period, a different country's law, one-sided NDAs that claim to be mutual, and clauses that try to stop us hiring their staff.

Each NDA was read by a fresh AI reviewer that knew nothing about the others, using the exact instructions the tool would send.

Does it look at everything?

On our template, every change the other side made is marked. That tells us exactly where the changes are, so we can check whether the tool commented on each one. A change with no comment is the most dangerous kind of mistake, because nobody would notice it.

The 42 changes the tool called fine still need a person to say whether "fine" was right. We have listed them for that.

Does it give the same answer twice?

We gave three of the NDAs to five fresh reviewers each, with identical instructions. Then we compared what they raised.

This is the clearest finding of the whole test, and the main reason for a trial before any real use. In Phase 2 we built and tested a fix: a fixed checklist, answered the same way every time.

Phase 2: making the review consistent →

Does the same clause get the same answer in different NDAs?

Not always. Three examples:

The clauseFound inTool said fineTool said fallbackTool said outside the rules
Proof of what you already knew must be "contemporaneous written records" (a stricter test than ours)180414
Contractors added to the people who may see our information10208
Insurers missing from the people we may share with12318

The fixed rules soften the first row: on a high-risk topic, a "fallback" answer goes to a lawyer anyway, so all 18 reached one. They cannot fix the "fine" answers in the other rows. For simple checks like "does this list include insurers?", we would take the AI out of the decision and let a plain rule check the list.

Smaller checks

  • Quotes are real. A script checks every quote against the contract. On the first pass 556 of 572 were exact; the same reviewer re-quoted the rest. Now all 572 pass. A real quote can still be beside the point: 18 quotes share no key words with their topic, and at least one is plainly wrong.
  • Reason and answer agree. In 1 finding the AI's reason said "a lawyer should look" while its answer said "fine". The fixed rules send it to a lawyer.
  • Drafts are real wording. 48 of 49 drafted fixes are wording a lawyer could send. 1 is an instruction ("reinstate the references") and is labelled as one.

The next test we would run

  1. The person who wrote the test contracts marks each change the tool called "fine" as right or wrong. That gives a first measure of how often it misses.
  2. Run all 30 through the finished tool, five times each, to measure consistency in the real set-up.
  3. In the trial, set a bar for each topic before it gets more automation, for example 95% agreement with lawyers over at least 100 decisions, and no missed high-risk issue in a random sample of the tool's "fine" calls. The lawyers set the final bar.

The full report, with every flagged finding, is in engine/evals/EVAL_REPORT.md.
