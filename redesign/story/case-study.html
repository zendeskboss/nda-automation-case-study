Could a tool read NDAs first?

Every NDA waits for a lawyer, even one that matches our own template word for word, and the deal waits with it. The goal: get NDAs signed faster and give lawyers time back for higher-value work, without losing control. An AI tool does the routine reading. Lawyers keep every decision.

The business closes deals faster

An NDA is often the first step of a deal. While it waits for a lawyer, the deal waits too. If the routine reading is done first, the NDA is signed sooner and the deal can move.

The short version

What we did
We wrote down the kind of rules a legal team uses to review an NDA. We built a small working tool that reads an NDA against those rules, and we ran it on 30 made-up NDAs. Then we tried to break it.
What we found
The basic idea works in the test: the tool sorts each clause into "fine", "fix drafted" or "a lawyer decides". But the AI is not consistent enough to trust on its own. The fixed rules around it matter more than the AI itself.
Then we fixed the main weakness
In Phase 2 we rebuilt the reading as a fixed checklist, then tuned it in Phase 2.1. The same NDA now raises the same issues 93% to 100% of the time, against 39% to 52% before. It sends more work to lawyers, and no lawyer has checked whether it is right.
What we would build
A tool that prepares each incoming NDA for a lawyer, so the lawyer reads only what needs a lawyer. We would start with a trial where the tool's view is recorded but the lawyers still decide everything.
What we cannot say yet
Whether the tool's calls are legally right. No lawyer has checked them. That is the first thing a real trial would measure.

Our assumptions during the study

This is a case study, with test NDAs. All rules are our assumptions, made with best efforts with no access to a legal team to verify. Any real world build would fully involve legal teams.

We can say the approach and safety design holds up in a small test, but we cannot say how often the tool is correct without a real world implementation.

Where we started

NDAs come in high volume and are mostly low risk. But each one waits in a queue for a lawyer, and the business waits with it. Much of a lawyer's time goes on clauses that match our template exactly.

So the goal was not "let an AI review NDAs". It was narrower: a lawyer should spend time only on the clauses that need a lawyer, and be able to trust that nothing was missed. The second half of that sentence turned out to be the hard part.

How we approached it

Three decisions shaped everything else. A fourth habit, testing, comes after the results.

1. Start with the lawyers, not the AI

The legal team's tracker lists the risks in an NDA, such as how long the duty of confidence lasts. It does not say where the line is: that 2 to 5 years is fine, 7 is not, and "forever" needs a lawyer. That knowledge sits in lawyers' heads. A tool can only follow rules that someone has written down, so the first job is a short worksheet that the lawyers fill in. We call the result the playbook.

2. Let the AI read, and let fixed rules decide

The AI is good at reading. It is not reliable enough to decide what a lawyer sees. So we split the work: the AI reads and gives a view, and a small set of fixed, testable rules in code puts each clause in one of four trays.

Follow one clause through the tool →

3. Decide, topic by topic, how far the tool may go

Not every topic carries the same risk. A wrong call on the notice address costs little. A wrong call on who owns the ideas shared under the NDA could cost a lot. So for each topic the lawyers choose one of three levels: the tool may draft a fix, it may form a view but keep it hidden until the lawyer decides, or it may only point at the clause.

What happened in the test (Phase 1)

Across the 30 made-up NDAs, the tool reported 572 clause-level findings. This is where the fixed rules sent them.

Phase 1 was good: the tool sorted every finding, and the fixed rules around it did their job. But it also showed a consistency problem. The same NDA, reviewed again, raised different issues. That forced us into Phase 2.

How we keep it safe →

From Phase 1 to Phase 2.1

Phase 1 showed that the idea works, and it showed the weak spot. Each later version fixed the main problem of the one before it.

Phase 1 to Phase 2. In Phase 1 the AI chose what to look for, so each review looked at different things.

  • First idea: have a second, different AI read every NDA too, to catch what the first one missed. We dropped it, because a second AI would still choose what to look for.
  • What we found: Jev, a model that can only pick from fixed answers, gave the same answer 97% of the time.
  • So we changed the plan: a fixed checklist decides what to check, and Jev answers the same questions for every clause.

Phase 2 to Phase 2.1. Phase 2 was far more consistent, but it sent 451 items to lawyers. Six changes, based on how the lawyers' rules work, cut that by 26%. Every tracked change from the other side still reaches a person.

Why Phase 2 sends more to lawyers. It checks more, and it plays safe:

  • It checks every clause, not only the ones the AI noticed.
  • It flags every change the other side asks for, and every missing topic.
  • When it is unsure, it asks a lawyer.

Some of the extra items are real issues that Phase 1 missed. Some are not needed. Only a lawyer can tell which. So we added a continuous improvement cycle to the system.

Still open. No version has been checked by a lawyer, so we cannot yet say which one is right more often.

Try to break it before anyone trusts it

A tool that looks right is not the same as a tool that is right. We spent as much time testing as building, and we ran every version through the same tests: the same NDA again to see if the answers change, misleading notes hidden inside contracts, and every quote checked against the real text.

How we tested it →

What we could build

What exists: the core of the tool. That is the rules, the reader, the sorter and a review screen.

What is still needed: the work around it, which is most of the effort.

  • Take in NDAs where they arrive today.
  • Read Word files with tracked changes.
  • Show the results where lawyers already work.

We would start with a trial in the background.

  • On high-risk topics, the lawyer decides without seeing the tool's view. Both answers are recorded.
  • A lawyer checks a random sample of the clauses the tool called "fine".
  • A topic gets more help from the tool only when the record shows it agrees with the lawyers often enough. The lawyers set that bar.
